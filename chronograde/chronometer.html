Chronometer standards, explained

Understand the rate ranges, the testing context and the limits of an app-based comparison.

A rate comparison is not a certificate

A chronometer certification records the outcome of a defined test programme. It is different from a chronograph, which is a stopwatch function. A daily rate inside a published range is useful context, but does not establish that a watch passed the rest of a certification programme.

COSC and the mechanical chronometer range

The widely quoted −4 to +6 seconds per day is a mean daily rate criterion for mechanical chronometer movements. It is not a promise that every individual reading, position or day on the wrist will fall within that range. Certification also involves controlled conditions and other criteria; the applicable movement category and test programme matter.

The Swiss Federal Institute of Metrology explains the distinction between ISO 3159 movement testing and Master Chronometer watch testing. A certificate describes the specified tests when they were performed. Age or a surprising reading alone does not establish a watch's present condition; compare repeatable measurements with the manufacturer's guidance for that particular watch.

METAS Master Chronometer

METAS-Cert provides independent certification covering the finished watch. The movement must first meet the ISO 3159 chronometer criteria. The programme also evaluates magnetic resistance, rate under different conditions, declared power reserve and water resistance.

The frequently quoted 0 to +5 s/day applies to category 1a. The published METAS-N001 requirements, version 1.2 (PDF) distinguish three movement-size categories:

METAS categoryAverage daily precision
1a0 to +5 s/day
1b0 to +6 s/day
20 to +7 s/day

Categories depend on the movement's fitting diameter or surface; see sections 4 and 7 of the requirements. The procedure includes exposure to a 1.5 tesla (15,000 gauss) magnetic field. Its tests cannot be replaced by a microphone estimate or a series of manual time readings.

Manufacturer standards are distinct

  • Grand Seiko. The mechanical Grand Seiko Standard uses six positions. Its published mean daily rate is −3 to +5 s/day for the regular category and −2 to +4 s/day for the Special Standard; smaller movements have a separate category. These controlled-test figures differ from the manufacturer's guidance for actual use.
  • Rolex Superlative Chronometer. Rolex describes official chronometer testing of the movement, followed by its own finished-watch precision assessment at −2 to +2 s/day. Its current certification explanation also covers requirements beyond rate.
  • Patek Philippe Seal. This is Patek Philippe's own standard for the complete watch. Its current published precision requirement is −1 to +2 seconds per 24 hours, assessed through production and on the assembled watch. It is a different programme from Rolex's certification. For an older watch, consult the specification applicable to its model and period.

What Chronograde can help you compare
