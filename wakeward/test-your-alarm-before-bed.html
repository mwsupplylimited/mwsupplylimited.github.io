Test your alarm before bed

You can verify most of an alarm by hand in about two minutes, and it is worth more than any setting you can inspect — because a test exercises the same path the real alarm will take, including the parts iOS will not report to anybody.

Set the conditions first

The point of the test is to reproduce 6:30am, not to prove the speaker works. Before you start, put the device into the state it will actually be in overnight:

  • The Ring/Silent switch (or the Action button) exactly where you leave it.
  • Sleep Focus, or whatever Focus turns on at night, on.
  • The ringer volume set where you want it — drag Settings › Sounds & Haptics › Ringtone and Alerts and listen to the sample, which plays at the exact volume your alarm will use.
  • The charging cable in, if that is the routine.
  • Headphones, watch and Bluetooth speaker connected or disconnected, as they will be.

Testing on an unlocked phone in your hand at full media volume tells you almost nothing.

The test

  1. Add a new alarm for two minutes from now in the app you actually rely on — the built-in Clock, or your alarm app. Use an alarm, not a timer and not the ringtone preview in Settings. You want the same mechanism your morning alarm uses, not something that resembles it.
  2. Give it the same sound as your real alarm. Tones differ enormously in how well they carry through a closed door.
  3. Lock the device and put it where it sleeps. The nightstand, the far side of the room, under a pillow, in a drawer. Where it sits changes the answer more than any setting on this page.
  4. Walk away. Go to where your head will be. Do not hold the phone and do not watch the screen.
  5. When it rings, do not look at it. On a Face ID iPhone, looking at the screen is itself a way to lower the alarm: iOS checks for attention before lowering the volume of some alerts. Listen first, from lying down, before you go and stop it.
  6. Stop it, then delete the test alarm. It is easy to leave behind and get a surprise from at 11pm.

What a pass looks like

  • A full-screen alert you have to act on — not a banner that disappears by itself. A banner means you are relying on a notification, which a Focus or the silent switch can silence.
  • Loud enough at the distance your head will be, through whatever the device is lying on and through a closed door if there is one.
  • You felt the vibration, if vibration is part of what you rely on.

If the sound failed, work through what silences an alarm. If it was audible but quieter than you set it, two settings move the alarm volume on their own.

What a test cannot prove

This is the honest part, and it is why a passed test is not the end of it.

  • That it is set for the right day. The most common cause of a missed alarm has nothing to do with sound. Check the repeat days and check AM/PM — a test alarm two minutes out proves neither.
  • That the device survives the night. A phone at 8% and unplugged is a silent alarm at 6:30, and it passed your test at 11pm.
  • That nothing moves before morning. If Change with Buttons is on, the volume you just tested can be changed by a button press at any point between now and then.
  • That iOS behaves. Alarms have been reported firing five to forty-five minutes late when scheduled a long way ahead, and iOS has shipped silent-alarm bugs in more than one year. A test tells you about the next two minutes, not the next eight hours.

Re-test after every iOS update

Settings genuinely move across major updates, and alarm behavior has changed across point releases. A test you passed two versions ago is not evidence about the version you are running now. The rule worth adopting is simple: re-run it whenever the iOS version changes, and once a month or so regardless. Anything older than about six weeks is a memory, not a check.

The layer a test cannot replace

Everything above verifies one alarm. It does not protect you on the morning that one alarm fails for a reason nobody could see — a glance at the screen, a button press in a pocket, a bad night, an iOS bug.
